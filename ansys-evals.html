Straight answers

Questions founders ask

Do I have to join a consortium to win an OTA?
For most DoD prototype OTAs, yes — the government awards a large OTA to a consortium manager, and only members can respond to the Requests for Prototype Proposals or Requests for Solutions issued under it. Joining is usually fast and cheap. A few OTAs are direct, but the consortium route is where the bulk of the accessible money lives.
How much does consortium membership cost?
It ranges from free (DIBC) to roughly $250–$1,000 a year for most (NSTXL tiers from ~$250; SOSSEC, TReX and MCDC around $500; MTEC ~$1,000 small / ~$5,000 large). MTEC also charges a 2% assessment on funded awards. One membership commonly unlocks a whole family of OTAs, so the fee is rarely the deciding factor — technology fit is.
Can a stealth or pre-incorporation team pursue this?
You can research everything now, and you should. But to register on SAM.gov for a CAGE code and UEI, and to sign a consortium membership agreement, you generally need a legal entity. Plan to incorporate before you'd actually accept an award. Nothing here is a guarantee of funding — it's a map of the path.
Is OTA money really non-dilutive?
Yes. An Other Transaction is a contract for a prototype, not an investment — the government funds the work and takes no equity. IP terms are negotiable, so you can often retain commercial rights. It behaves very differently from venture capital, and many startups use it precisely to build without giving up ownership.
What actually makes a white paper win?
Specificity and feasibility. Reviewers reward a sharp understanding of their problem, a concrete technical approach, a believable team and schedule, and a real transition story — plus hard evidence the physics works. A single credible simulation result often separates a funded white paper from a hopeful one.
