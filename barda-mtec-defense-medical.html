BARDA & MTEC: the preparedness and defense path

Updated July 29, 2026 · Free educational guide · verify details at the official sources below

BARDA DRIVe funds early health-security tech through the EZ-BAA (up to ~$750K, fast, rolling) and scales via the larger DRIVe BAA (up to ~$20M+). MTEC, the Army/DHA medical-technology consortium, awards $50K–$25M through Other Transactions for medical devices — you join the consortium and respond to Requests for Project Proposals.

Why look past NIH

NIH is the anchor, but a device that touches emergency preparedness, austere care, or military medicine has two more well-funded doors — and both move faster than a grant cycle.

BARDA DRIVe

BARDA sits inside HHS and funds medical countermeasures and health-security technology. Its DRIVe accelerator runs two main on-ramps. The EZ-BAA is deliberately lightweight — a short proposal for up to roughly $750K in non-dilutive funding, accepted on a rolling basis against defined areas of interest like early infection detection and sepsis. If your program outgrows that, the larger DRIVe BAA scales well past $20M with BARDA's advanced-development support attached. Spectral AI's DeepView burn-imaging device is a real example of the BARDA-funded device path.

If your project needs simulation

Defense and preparedness reviewers want field-ready hardware. Simulation of ruggedness, thermal limits, and reliability is exactly the evidence they look for — ask us about an Ansys eval.

MTEC: the defense-to-device bridge

The Medical Technology Enterprise Consortium is a nonprofit that channels Army USAMRDC and Defense Health Agency dollars into medical technology through Other Transaction agreements. Awards run anywhere from $50K to $25M; recent device-focused Requests for Project Proposals (RPPs) have averaged around $2M–$3.5M over two to three years. Because it's an OT vehicle, MTEC moves faster and collaborates more freely than a classic contract.

How to get in

MTEC works on membership. Join the consortium — it's inexpensive and open to small businesses, large companies, universities, and nonprofits — then monitor the member portal for RPPs and respond with a white paper to any that match. Membership also plugs you into investor and partner matchmaking across the defense-health ecosystem.

What 'dual-use' really means for you

Both agencies fund technology that serves a mission — pandemic response for BARDA, warfighter and casualty care for MTEC — but the resulting devices almost always have civilian markets too. A hemorrhage-control device, a field-portable diagnostic, a ruggedized monitor: each solves a military or preparedness problem and a hospital or EMS problem. Framing your device as genuinely dual-use widens your funding options and strengthens the commercialization story you'll later tell NIH or investors. Reviewers can tell the difference between a real dual-use case and a civilian device wearing camouflage, so make it honest.

The consortium advantage

Joining MTEC does more than unlock RPPs. You gain visibility into what the Defense Health Agency is actually prioritizing, access to matchmaking with primes and other members, and a channel to program stakeholders you'd otherwise never reach. For a small team, that market intelligence is often worth as much as the award itself.

Stack the doors

These aren't mutually exclusive with NIH or ARPA-H. Many device teams sequence them: an NIH SBIR proves feasibility, a BARDA or MTEC award funds the field-ready build. Defense and preparedness reviewers care intensely about ruggedness and reliability — the kind of thing you prove with thermal, vibration, and physics-of-failure simulation long before a device ships.

Official sources: BARDA DRIVe — EZ-BAA · MTEC (Medical Technology Enterprise Consortium). Figures change; confirm on the official page before relying on them.
