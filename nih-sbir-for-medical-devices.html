NIH SBIR for medical devices: the non-dilutive playbook

Updated July 29, 2026 · Free educational guide · verify details at the official sources below

NIH SBIR/STTR is the largest source of non-dilutive funding for U.S. health small businesses — roughly $1.3B a year — and medical devices are fully eligible. A Phase I award runs about $314K–$323K for feasibility; Phase II reaches roughly $2.15M for development. You apply to a specific NIH institute against standard January, April, and September due dates.

Why NIH is the anchor

If you build medical hardware — imaging, surgical robotics, drug-delivery devices, implants — NIH is almost always the first non-dilutive stop. The agency pushes on the order of $1.3B a year into small businesses through SBIR and STTR, and unlike a VC term sheet, it takes none of your equity.

The money, honestly

Under the current budget guidance, a Phase I award lands around $314K–$323K and buys you 6–12 months to prove feasibility. Phase II reaches roughly $2.15M over two years to build and test a prototype. Certain topics carry waivers that allow more, and there is now a Phase IIB 'Strategic Breakthrough' track that can scale follow-on support dramatically for technologies that already cleared Phase II. Treat the headline numbers as ceilings, not entitlements.

Pick the institute before the idea gets cold

NIH is not one funder — it's dozens. A cardiac device belongs at NHLBI; imaging hardware at NIBIB; an oncology tool at NCI; a neuro-device at NINDS or the BRAIN Initiative. Each institute has its own priorities and program officers. Email the program officer early; a ten-minute call shapes whether you respond to the broad omnibus or a targeted funding opportunity.

If your project needs simulation

Before you write the Phase I aims, sketch the physics you'll need to prove. An Ansys evaluation and a short call with an engineer can turn a vague 'we'll model it' into a concrete, fundable plan.

Deadlines and mechanics

Standard SBIR/STTR due dates fall around January 5, April 5, and September 5. Before you can submit you need registrations — SAM.gov, eRA Commons, an SBA company registry entry — and those take weeks, so start now, not the week before. Submission is through Grants.gov or ASSIST.

Where simulation earns its keep

Reviewers reward de-risking. A Phase I that says 'we will characterize stresses' is weaker than one that shows an early finite-element or CFD result and a plan to validate it. Since the FDA now has a formal framework for trusting computational modeling in submissions, building your verification-and-validation story during Phase I does double duty: it strengthens the grant and it front-loads regulatory evidence. SBIR versus STTR — a quick note

They look identical from the outside, but STTR requires a formal partnership with a research institution that performs at least 30% of the work, while SBIR keeps the bulk of the work inside your company. If your device grew out of a university lab and you still lean on that lab's facilities, STTR often fits better; if you've already pulled the core engineering in-house, SBIR is cleaner. Both carry the same money and the same due dates, so choose based on where the real work happens.

Don't sleep on the ecosystem programs

NIH runs more than grants. REACH hubs fund the earliest proof-of-concept work for academic teams, and the SEED office publishes plain-language guides that are genuinely useful. Use them — a founder who has clearly read the room writes a stronger application.

A realistic first move

Do customer discovery through I-Corps at NIH, line up your institute and program officer, and draft specific aims that a physics model can support. That combination — clear clinical need, right institute, de-risked engineering — is what separates a funded device from a filed one.

Official sources: NIH SEED (SBIR/STTR home) · NIDA SBIR/STTR award budget. Figures change; confirm on the official page before relying on them.
