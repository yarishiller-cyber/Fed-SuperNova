Simulation for energy hardware

Updated July 29, 2026 · Free educational guide · verify details at the official sources below

Energy hardware fails on physics — heat, stress, flow, and fields. Engineering simulation lets you model thermal runaway, power-electronics heating, turbine aerodynamics, and fusion magnet loads before building anything, so you iterate in software instead of blowing up prototypes. It also makes funding proposals more credible by backing performance claims with modeling.

Energy hardware is a physics problem

Almost every energy technology lives or dies on the same handful of physics: heat, mechanical stress, fluid flow, and electromagnetic fields. A battery pack that thermally runs away, an inverter that cooks its own switches, a turbine blade that fatigues, a fusion magnet that can't hold its own forces — these are simulation problems long before they're funding problems.

What you can model before you build

Take the big categories. Batteries: cell-to-cell thermal runaway and pack cooling, in Fluent and Mechanical. Power electronics: electrothermal behavior of SiC/GaN modules, in Icepak, SIwave, and Maxwell. Fusion: magnet forces and structural loads, in Maxwell and Mechanical. Wind and turbomachinery: aerodynamics and blade fatigue, in CFX, Fluent, and Mechanical. Carbon capture and hydrogen: reactor and stack fluid dynamics, in Fluent.

Why it changes the build loop

Physical prototypes are slow and expensive, especially in energy, where a test rig can cost more than a year of payroll. Simulation lets you explore dozens of designs in software, kill the bad ones early, and reserve physical testing for the few candidates worth the money. You don't replace testing — you aim it.

Why funders care

There's a second payoff. When an ARPA-E concept paper or an SBIR proposal states a performance target, reviewers ask whether it's physically real. A modeling result inside the proposal answers that question before it's asked. It signals technical maturity — that you understand your own physics well enough to have already simulated it.
