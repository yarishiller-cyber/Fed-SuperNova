ARPA-H: how to apply for the 'grants' that aren't grants

Updated July 29, 2026 · Free educational guide · verify details at the official sources below

ARPA-H funds device innovation mainly through Other Transactions and cooperative agreements — not classic grants — plus an SBIR with the largest caps anywhere: up to ~$600K Phase I and ~$3.5M Phase II. You apply by responding to a specific program solicitation or the open BAA on SAM.gov, usually with a short summary first, then a full proposal if invited.

What ARPA-H actually is

ARPA-H is the 'DARPA for health' — a young HHS agency built to fund high-risk, high-reward breakthroughs that the traditional grant machinery is too cautious to touch. That mission changes how the money moves.

The mechanisms confuse people

Most ARPA-H funding is not a grant. It flows through Other Transactions (OTs) and cooperative agreements awarded against mission-focused program solicitations. OTs are flexible contracts — fewer of the rigid rules that govern grants, more negotiation, and aggressive go/no-go milestones. If you're used to NIH's R-series culture, this feels foreign; plan for a program manager who behaves like an activist investor, not a passive funder.

The SBIR is the friendliest door

ARPA-H also runs an SBIR/STTR program with the most generous caps in the federal system: up to roughly $600K for Phase I and $3.5M for Phase II, issued as milestone-based contracts. For a device startup, that's a meaningfully bigger runway than a standard NIH SBIR, in exchange for hitting ambitious checkpoints on schedule.

If your project needs simulation

ARPA-H rewards ambitious, milestone-driven proposals. Simulation is the cheapest way to show an aggressive Phase II milestone is physically reachable — ask us about an Ansys eval while you scope the work.

How to actually apply

Watch two places: the ARPA-H funding pages and SAM.gov, where solicitations live. For most programs you submit a short Solution Summary or abstract against a specific topic; if it resonates, you're invited to a full proposal. Attend the Proposers' Day for any program you're serious about — it's where you learn what the program manager actually wants and where teaming happens.

Use the network

ARPA-H built ARPANET-H, a set of regional hubs, to help performers who aren't already wired into Boston or the Bay. The Investor Catalyst Hub (run by VentureWell) connects SBIR performers to investors and pilot sites. If you're a stealth or first-time team, these hubs shorten the distance to a warm introduction.

What OTs mean for your paperwork and IP

Because Other Transactions sit outside the standard Federal Acquisition Regulation, the terms are negotiated rather than boilerplate — which cuts both ways. You get flexibility on intellectual property, deliverables, and milestones that a rigid grant wouldn't allow, but you also need someone on your side who can read a term sheet. Don't sign an OT the way you'd accept a grant; treat it like a contract negotiation, because that's what it is.

Timelines are unforgiving

ARPA-H's whole design philosophy is speed with hard go/no-go gates. Miss a milestone and funding can stop — that's the deal in exchange for the generous caps. Build schedule risk into your plan honestly, and don't promise a Phase II deliverable you can only hit if everything breaks your way. Reviewers have seen the optimistic Gantt chart before.

Make the ambition credible

ARPA-H says yes to bold. The trap is that 'bold' reads as 'unfounded' unless you show the physics could work. A first-pass simulation — hemodynamics, field strength, thermal dose — is the cheapest evidence that your aggressive milestone is reachable. Pair that with our selection guide before you write.

Official sources: ARPA-H — For Small Businesses (SBIR) · ARPA-H — Open Funding Opportunities. Figures change; confirm on the official page before relying on them.
