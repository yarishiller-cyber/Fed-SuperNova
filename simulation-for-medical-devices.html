Simulation for medical devices: physics by device type

Updated July 29, 2026 · Free educational guide · verify details at the official sources below

Medical-device simulation splits by physics: structural FEA for implants and stents (fatigue, deployment), CFD for blood pumps, valves, and inhalers (flow, hemolysis, deposition), electromagnetics for MRI safety and antennas, and thermal/reliability for implanted electronics. Ansys tools map onto each, and since the FDA's 2023 guidance, credible models count as submission evidence.

Simulation isn't one thing

'We'll simulate it' is meaningless until you name the physics. Medical devices span four broad domains, and the right tool — and the right funding pitch — depends on which one dominates your risk.

Structural (implants, stents, delivery systems)

Anything that bears mechanical load needs finite-element analysis. Stent deployment, recoil, and fatigue over millions of cardiac cycles; catheter and delivery-system flexibility; implant fixation. Ansys Mechanical handles the stress and fatigue; LS-DYNA covers the fast, nonlinear events like crimping and balloon expansion. This is the most mature FEA claim reviewers see.

Fluids (pumps, valves, drug delivery)

Blood-contacting and airway devices live in CFD. Ansys Fluent predicts pressure-flow performance and shear-driven hemolysis in blood pumps, leaflet flow in heart valves (coupled with Mechanical for fluid-structure interaction), and droplet deposition in inhalers and nasal sprays. The FDA has even published benchmark CFD problems for blood-device flows.

Electromagnetics (imaging, wireless, neurostim)

High-frequency EM — Ansys HFSS — covers implantable and wearable antennas, telemetry links, and, critically, MRI RF-induced heating of implants (the analysis behind an 'MR Conditional' label). Low-frequency EM — Ansys Maxwell — shapes neurostimulation fields and motor design. Pair HFSS with a thermal solve when you need worst-case tissue temperature.

Thermal & reliability (implanted electronics)

An implantable pulse generator or wearable is an electronics reliability problem. Ansys Icepak handles cooling and thermal management; Sherlock predicts solder-joint fatigue and physics-of-failure; SIwave and Q3D keep the signal clean on dense sensor boards.

Multiphysics is where devices actually live

Real devices rarely respect these tidy boxes. An MRI-safe implant is an electromagnetic and thermal problem. A heart valve is fluid and structural — the leaflets deform the flow and the flow loads the leaflets, so you couple Fluent and Mechanical into a fluid-structure interaction. A surgical robot arm is structural, thermal, and electromagnetic at once. The value of a single simulation platform is that you can hand data between physics without exporting to a dozen disconnected tools and losing fidelity at every step.

Materials are the quiet failure point

Every FEA and fatigue result rests on material data, and 'we used a handbook nitinol curve' is where a lot of credibility cases wobble. Nitinol, medical-grade titanium, PEEK, and silicones all behave in ways generic properties miss. A managed materials source like Ansys Granta keeps your inputs traceable so a reviewer's question about provenance has a clean answer.

Tie it back to money and the FDA

Every one of these de-risks a funding milestone and, done to V&V 40 credibility, becomes evidence in your submission. Start with the physics that carries your biggest technical risk, prove it early in Phase I, and build from there.
