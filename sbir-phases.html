SBIR Phase I, II and III, explained with 2026 numbers.

Updated July 28, 2026 · Free educational guide · verify details at the official sources linked below

SBIR has three phases: Phase I proves feasibility (up to ~$323,090 in 2026, 6–12 months), Phase II builds the prototype (up to ~$2,153,927, ~24 months), and Phase III is commercialization — no SBIR dollars, but agencies may buy from you sole-source. Direct-to-Phase-II lets qualified teams skip Phase I entirely.

Phase I: prove it's feasible

Phase I funds a feasibility study, not a product. The 2026 SBA guideline lets agencies award up to about $323,090 without special approval, but actual awards vary widely: NSF funds up to ~$305K over 6–18 months, NIH around the guideline (and above it for approved topics), DOE $200–250K, NASA $150–225K over 6 months, many DoD topics $100–250K, and some Air Force open topics have historically run smaller and faster. Expect 6–12 months of performance, and expect to do at least two-thirds of the work in-house.

What wins Phase I is credible evidence the physics works and a team that can execute — see how proposals are scored.

Phase II: build the prototype

Phase II is the real R&D money: currently up to about $2,153,927 under the SBA guideline, typically over 24 months, to take the Phase I result to a working prototype. Agencies weigh your commercialization plan heavily here. Roughly 40% of Phase I awardees convert to Phase II. Several agencies stack more on top: NSF's Phase IIB supplement matches third-party investment, NIH funds Phase IIB continuations, NASA offers Phase II-E/II-X extensions, and DoD writes sequential Phase IIs.

Phase III: the part nobody explains

Phase III involves no SBIR money at all. It's any follow-on work that "derives from, extends, or completes" your SBIR project, paid with regular procurement or private dollars. Two things make it valuable: agencies may award Phase III contracts to you sole-source, with no further competition, and your SBIR data rights protect your technical data for 20 years. In defense, that combination is why primes and program offices take SBIR-funded startups seriously. But it's a right, not a promise — you still need a customer with budget.

If your project needs simulation

Phase I is a feasibility study on a short clock, and at many agencies the core deliverable is analysis: CFD, FEA, thermal, or electromagnetic modeling that shows the concept holds. Teams that already have simulation running on day one spend the award proving, not tooling up. Ask us what an Ansys evaluation could cover for your Phase I workload.

The shortcuts: D2P2, Fast-Track, TABA

  • Direct-to-Phase-II (D2P2): skip Phase I if you can document Phase-I-level feasibility funded by other money. Offered by DoD (AFWERX runs D2P2s up to ~$2M, requiring a signed Air Force customer memo), NIH (SBIR only), DOE, and Education. NSF doesn't offer it.
  • Fast-Track (NIH): submit Phase I and II together, reviewed once, closing the funding gap between phases.
  • TABA: extra money on top of your award for commercialization help — up to $6,500 per Phase I and $50,000 per Phase II project under current policy — for market research, IP strategy, and regulatory planning. Ask for it; many first-timers don't.

How long does it all take?

Agency pathDecide → first dollar (realistic)
DoD (fastest paths: AFWERX open topics)~5–9 months
NSF (Project Pitch → invite → award)~7–10 months
NIH (study-section review cycle)~9–14 months

Plan cash accordingly, and start registrations at least eight weeks before any deadline — the full chain is in the registration checklist.
