Who gets accepted by AFWERX?

Updated July 29, 2026 · Free educational guide · verify details at the official sources below

AFWERX rewards genuine dual-use technology with a clear defense use case, a credible team, and — increasingly — an identified Air or Space Force customer and a realistic path to transition. In 2026 the shift toward focused open topics means alignment to a named mission gap matters more than a generic dual-use story. Eligibility is a US small business under 500 employees, majority US-owned.

The selection isn't a lottery

It can feel like one, but there's a pattern to what wins. At the earliest stage the bar is feasibility and dual-use fit: is this real, and can it serve both a commercial and a defense market? As you move up the ladder, the questions get harder and more about transition than technology.

What reviewers reward

A real customer. The single strongest signal is an Air or Space Force end user who wants the capability. A signed Customer Memorandum can even let you skip Phase I via Direct-to-Phase-II.

Tight topic alignment. With the 2026 move to focused open topics, a proposal that precisely answers a named mission gap beats a broader, vaguer one — even if the vaguer one has flashier technology.

A credible path to scale. Especially for TACFI and STRATFI, reviewers want to see committed matching money and a believable route to a program of record.

What sinks good technology

Great tech loses when there's no identified customer, when the dual-use story is an afterthought, or when the team can't articulate how the SBIR contract leads anywhere. The government is buying a path to a capability, not a demo. Another quiet killer is eligibility: majority US-ownership and control is a hard requirement, and post-2026 reauthorization the foreign-investment screening got stricter — a cap table that looked fine two years ago may need a second look.

The selection is a funnel, not a gate

It helps to think of acceptance as a series of increasingly demanding checkpoints rather than one yes/no. Phase I asks 'is this feasible and dual-use?' Phase II asks 'can you build it, and does a customer want it?' TACFI and STRATFI ask 'will others put money behind it, and is there a program of record at the end?' The Strategic Breakthrough tier asks 'is this important enough to bet up to $30M on?' Each rung filters harder on transition and less on novelty. Founders who map their evidence to the checkpoint they're actually at — instead of over-selling the technology at every stage — tend to convert better.

If your project needs simulation

A concrete engineering artifact — a simulation showing your design meets a hard requirement — is one of the most persuasive things you can put in front of a technical reviewer.

How to stack the odds

Start customer conversations before you write a word of the proposal. Mirror the topic's language. And bring evidence — a prototype, test data, or credible analysis — that de-risks the claim you're making. Then map your ladder: apply to Open Topic, then plan the STRATFI/TACFI transition early.
