ISS National Lab & in-space manufacturing: where the money actually is

Updated July 29, 2026 · Free educational guide · verify details at the official sources below

The ISS National Lab (operated by CASIS) is your route to running R&D and manufacturing experiments in orbit — often with funding attached. It runs technology-advancement solicitations (recent rounds up to ~$650K), co-funds fundamental science with NSF, and runs the Orbital Edge Accelerator that pairs startups with $500K–$750K in private capital plus a Boeing-sponsored prize of up to $100K. Most tracks use a two-step process: a short concept summary first, full proposal by invitation.

Microgravity does things you cannot buy on the ground — it grows better protein crystals, pulls fiber with fewer defects, and lets fluids and biology behave in ways that unlock new products. The ISS National Lab exists to get commercial and academic R&D up there, and for space-hardware founders the important part is that it frequently brings funding with it.

Who runs it. The National Lab portion of the International Space Station is managed by CASIS (the Center for the Advancement of Science in Space) on NASA's behalf. It is the front door for non-NASA users who want to fly research or manufacturing hardware to station, and it partners with agencies and companies to put real dollars behind the best concepts.

The money on the table. Recent cycles show the shape of it. The National Lab has run technology-advancement solicitations offering up to roughly $650,000 for flight projects in areas like in-space servicing, assembly, and manufacturing. It co-funds fundamental physical-science research with the National Science Foundation — a recent NSF/CASIS transport-phenomena solicitation put multiple millions of dollars behind ISS research, with a feasibility-review step in January 2026 and full proposals in early March 2026. And its Orbital Edge Accelerator 2026 cohort includes a Disrupt Track for in-space manufacturing and space-based biology, pairing selected startups with $500,000–$750,000 in private capital from an investor consortium, plus eligibility for a Boeing-sponsored Technology in Space Prize worth up to $100,000 in non-dilutive grant funding.

If your project needs simulation

The two-step gate saves you weeks. Nearly every ISS National Lab opportunity screens a short Step 1 concept summary before inviting a full proposal. Nail the concept summary — clear in-orbit rationale, clear commercial or scientific payoff — and you're invited to write the long version. Skip straight to a 40-page proposal and there's nowhere to send it.

How the process works. Most ISS National Lab opportunities are two-step. You first submit a short concept summary — a few pages arguing why the work needs microgravity and what the payoff is. If it clears review, you're invited to submit a full proposal. This gates your effort sensibly: you learn whether the idea has legs before you sink weeks into a detailed submission. The accelerator and NSF tracks each have their own timelines, so watch the opportunities page rather than assuming one calendar covers everything.

What reviewers reward. Two things above all: a genuine microgravity rationale (an experiment that could run just as well on the ground will not compete), and a credible path to value — a product, a dataset, or a capability someone will pay for. For in-space production specifically, NASA and CASIS are actively trying to seed a commercial market ahead of future commercial stations, so a clear commercialization story is a feature, not a distraction.

Where to start. Read the current ISS National Lab funding opportunities and the In-Space Production Applications overview to see which track fits your hardware — then get a concept summary in front of them.

Official sources: ISS National Lab — Funding Opportunities · ISS National Lab — In-Space Production Applications. Figures change; confirm on the official page before relying on them.
