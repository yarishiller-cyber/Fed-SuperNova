Does NASA own your invention? The truth about SBIR data rights.

Updated July 29, 2026 · Free educational guide · verify details at the official sources below

The fear is understandable: you take government money, and the government takes your technology. For NASA SBIR and STTR, that fear is backwards. The program was built to help small companies commercialize, and the data-rights rules are the mechanism that makes it safe.

The fear is understandable: you take government money, and the government takes your technology. For NASA SBIR and STTR, that fear is backwards. The program was built to help small companies commercialize, and the data-rights rules are the mechanism that makes it safe.

You keep title to your inventions

Under the Bayh-Dole framework, a small business that invents something under an SBIR award retains title to that invention. NASA receives a paid-up, government-purpose license, it can use the technology for federal purposes, but it does not own your patent and cannot take your company's commercial rights. You are free to patent, license, and sell to commercial and other customers.

SBIR data rights: the 20-year shield

The bigger protection is SBIR data rights. Technical data you generate under the award, your designs, test results, know-how, is marked with an SBIR legend and protected for a period of roughly 20 years from award. During that window the government cannot release that data outside the government or hand it to a competitor. This is what stops a prime contractor from getting your hard-won engineering data for free.

If your project needs simulation

Plain version: You own the invention. NASA gets a license to use it, not to sell it or give it away. And your technical data stays protected for about 20 years, which is exactly what lets a Phase III contract be handed straight to you.

Why this makes SBIR non-dilutive in the deepest sense

Venture capital costs you equity. A strategic partner might cost you exclusivity or control. SBIR costs you neither, no equity, no board seat, no cost share, and you walk away owning the invention and holding the data rights. It is close to the only funding on earth that hands you money and lets you keep everything.

The payoff: sole-source Phase III

Data rights also power the best-kept secret in the program: Phase III. Because your Phase I/II work is protected and traceable to you, an agency or prime that wants the matured technology can award you a Phase III contract sole-source, no open competition, no dollar limit, no cost share. Your data rights are precisely what make that direct award defensible. In effect, the IP protection converts into a procurement advantage.

What founders should actually do

  • Mark everything. Apply the SBIR data-rights legend correctly to deliverables, an unmarked deliverable can lose protection.
  • Report inventions on time. Bayh-Dole title is conditional on disclosing inventions within the required window; miss it and you can forfeit title.
  • Don't hand data to primes casually. Your rights are strong on paper but easy to waive in a sloppy teaming agreement.
  • Track your Phase III lineage. Document how later work derives from the SBIR so a sole-source award is clean.

The headline for a space-hardware founder is simple: NASA SBIR does not take your equity and does not take your invention. Handled correctly, it funds your technology, protects your data, and hands you a direct path to a real contract. Read the official terms at nasa.gov/sbir_sttr before you sign.
