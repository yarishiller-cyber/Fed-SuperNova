How APFIT selection works: who gets funded

Updated July 29, 2026 · Free educational guide · verify details at the official sources below

APFIT projects are nominated by DoD program offices and components, then evaluated and down-selected centrally. Winning projects generally have a mature, demonstrated technology (roughly TRL 8+), a clear DoD end user who wants it now, a credible production and transition plan, and an award within the ~$50M statutory cap. FY26 funded 14 initial and 24 final projects, averaging over $30M.

Two gates, not one

Getting APFIT money means clearing two very different gates. The first is nomination: a DoD program office or component decides your project is worth putting forward. The second is selection: the nominated projects compete centrally, and a subset get funded. Founders obsess over the second gate, but the first is where most fail — because they never build the sponsor relationship at all.

What a sponsor needs from you

Program offices nominate projects that make them look good and solve a real problem. That means your technology should be mature — demonstrated, roughly TRL 8+, not a lab result. There should be a clear end user who wants quantities now. And you should hand your sponsor a clean story: what it does, why it can't wait for a program of record, how you'll produce it, and what the units cost.

What selection weighs

Once nominated, evaluators look at operational impact, technical maturity, the strength of the transition plan (does this actually lead somewhere after the buy?), and fit within the funding envelope — awards run up to the ~$50M statutory cap and averaged over $30M in FY26. The FY26 cycle funded 14 projects in its initial round and 24 in the final round, and debuted software capabilities for the first time.

The mistakes that sink nominations

A few patterns show up again and again. The first is pitching too early — bringing a TRL 5 lab result to a program office that needs something it can field this year. APFIT is a fielding tool, not a research grant; if your tech isn't essentially ready to produce, you're pitching the wrong program. The second is having no named end user. 'Someone in the DoD will want this' is not a transition plan. The third is a production story that falls apart under one question: can you actually build the quantity you're proposing, on schedule, at the unit cost you quoted?

The last one is where a lot of good companies stumble. It's easy to demonstrate one unit. It's much harder to convince a sponsor that units three hundred through five hundred will pass the same acceptance tests. That's a manufacturing and qualification argument, and it's exactly the argument simulation lets you make with evidence instead of optimism.

How to position

Finish a strong Phase II or prototype effort, get real end-user pull on record, and time your outreach to the component's nomination cycle. Bring a production-readiness package — including a simulation-backed qualification and yield story — so your sponsor is confident the buy will field, not stall. Meet sponsors where they are: service industry days, SOF Week, and the DIB Accelerator.

Official sources: APFIT program office · War Department: first FY26 APFIT round. Figures change; confirm on the official page before relying on them.
