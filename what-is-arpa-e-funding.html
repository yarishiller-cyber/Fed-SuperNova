ARPA-E: energy moonshots, funded with grants

Updated July 29, 2026 · Free educational guide · verify details at the official sources below

ARPA-E (the Advanced Research Projects Agency-Energy, part of the U.S. Department of Energy) funds transformative, high-risk energy technologies that are too early or too risky for private investors. It gives non-dilutive grants — typically $250K to $10M, around $3M on average — and takes no equity, usually with a modest cost share.

The point of ARPA-E

ARPA-E exists to fund the energy ideas that are too risky for a venture fund and too applied for a basic-science grant. Modeled on DARPA, it backs transformative concepts — not incremental improvements — with the expectation that many will fail and a few will change the game.

It's part of the U.S. Department of Energy, but it runs its own way: small, active program directors who push teams toward hard technical milestones.

What it actually funds

Money flows through two main channels. Focused programs target a specific challenge — long-duration storage, fusion materials, industrial heat — and a handful launch each year. OPEN solicitations are technology-agnostic: any transformative energy idea, run roughly every three years. On top of those sit SCALEUP (for prior awardees scaling up) and IGNIITE (for individual early-career innovators).

How much, and what strings

Individual awards typically run $250K to $10M, with about $3M being common, over periods up to 36 months. It's non-dilutive — ARPA-E takes no equity — though there's usually a cost share in the 5–20% range depending on who you are and what you're building. The funding is a grant or cooperative agreement, not a contract, so you keep control of your invention (subject to standard government march-in and reporting terms).

Who should apply

Startups, small businesses, universities, and national labs are all eligible, and teaming is encouraged. You don't need to be a big company — but you do need a genuinely transformative idea and a credible technical plan to prove it. If your pitch is "10% better," ARPA-E is the wrong door. If it's "a new physical approach that could reset the cost curve," it's exactly right.

The catch reviewers care about

ARPA-E reviewers are technical. They will ask whether your target is physically achievable, and "we think so" is not an answer. This is why teams increasingly bring simulation into the proposal itself — a thermal model, a stress estimate, a CFD sweep — to show the target isn't just optimistic. Start with the concept paper.
