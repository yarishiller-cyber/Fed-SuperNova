FDA V&V40: why the FDA now wants your simulation

Updated July 29, 2026 · Free educational guide · verify details at the official sources below

In November 2023 the FDA's CDRH finalized guidance on 'Assessing the Credibility of Computational Modeling and Simulation in Medical Device Submissions,' built on the ASME V&V 40 standard. It gives reviewers a risk-informed framework to trust a physics model as evidence in a 510(k), De Novo, or PMA — so credible simulation is now something the FDA actively expects, not merely tolerates.

The shift, in one sentence

Simulation used to be something you did privately and hoped a reviewer wouldn't poke at. As of the November 2023 CDRH guidance, it's a recognized category of regulatory evidence with a defined way to be judged. That flips the incentive: done right, a model helps you clear the FDA rather than raising questions.

What V&V 40 actually is

ASME V&V 40 is an FDA-recognized standard that answers a deceptively hard question: how much should we trust this model? Its core idea is credibility proportional to risk. If a simulation result drives a high-consequence decision — say, whether an implant survives ten years of loading — it must be validated far more rigorously than a model informing a low-stakes design tweak. You establish the model's 'question of interest,' its influence on the decision, and the consequence of being wrong; that combination sets how much verification and validation evidence you owe.

What the guidance asks for

The 2023 guidance lays out a credibility assessment: verification (are you solving the equations right?), validation (are you solving the right equations, checked against physical data?), and applicability (does the validation evidence cover the way you're actually using the model?). It applies across submission types — 510(k), De Novo, PMA, HDE, IDE — for physics-based models of stress, flow, thermal, and electromagnetic behavior.

Why this is the founder's angle

Here's the part most teams miss: the FDA doesn't just permit simulation, it now has infrastructure that rewards it. Through the Medical Device Development Tool (MDDT) program you can qualify a model once and reuse it across submissions. And in-silico trials let virtual patients supplement clinical enrollment. A funded device startup now has a regulatory reason to invest in simulation — not just an engineering preference.

A practical credibility checklist

When you sit down to build the case, the guidance effectively asks you to answer, in writing: What decision does this model inform, and how bad is it if the model is wrong? What did you do to verify the numerics (mesh convergence, solver checks)? What physical data did you validate against, and how close was the match? And does that validation actually cover your intended use — same loads, same geometry range, same materials? If you can answer those four cleanly, you have the skeleton of a credible submission.

The recognized-software advantage

Reviewers move faster when the underlying solver is one they already trust. Using FDA-recognized, widely validated tools doesn't grant automatic credibility — you still owe the V&V evidence — but it removes an argument you'd otherwise have to win from scratch about whether the software itself is sound.

Start the credibility case early

Build the V&V plan during Phase I, when it's cheap, using software the FDA already recognizes. Retrofitting credibility onto a model the week before a submission is how good devices get delayed. See our simulation overview for the physics-by-device-type breakdown.
