How to Write a Winning DIU Solution Brief

Updated July 29, 2026 · Free educational guide · verify details at the official sources below

A DIU solution brief is a short (usually 5-10 page) commercial pitch, not a FAR proposal. Lead with problem-solution fit against the specific Area of Interest, prove commercial traction and dual-use readiness, and make the transition path obvious. DIU typically responds within about 30 days on whether it wants a pitch.

The Defense Innovation Unit runs on Commercial Solutions Openings (CSOs), and the front door is the solution brief — a short document (usually 5–10 pages) you submit against a specific Area of Interest. Get it right and DIU invites you to pitch, typically within about 30 days. Get it wrong and you never hear back. The single biggest mistake founders make is writing it like a traditional FAR proposal. It is not. It is a commercial pitch aimed at a government buyer who is in a hurry.

Match one Area of Interest, exactly

Do not submit a generic capabilities deck. Open the live solicitation and mirror its language. If the problem statement asks for a counter-UAS sensor under a cost target, your first paragraph should name that sensor, that mission, and that number. Evaluators score whether your solution actually addresses the stated need — make it impossible to miss.

The winning structure

  • Problem-solution fit (page 1): restate the military problem in one sentence, then your solution in one sentence.
  • The product, today: what exists now, at what TRL, with screenshots or test data — not a roadmap of what you will build with their money.
  • Dual-use traction: commercial customers, revenue or fundraising to date, and why the commercial market keeps you alive between contracts.
  • Adaptation plan: the small delta to make your commercial product mission-ready. DIU funds adaptation, not ground-up R&D.
  • Transition path: who the end user is and how this scales to a production Other Transaction.
If your project needs simulation

Screen-out killers: writing like a FAR proposal, no commercial traction, no named military problem, and burying the ask. DIU reads dozens of briefs per Area of Interest — if your first page does not connect a real product to the stated need, you are out.

Prove it works before the pitch

DIU's whole thesis is speed and de-risking. Anything you can show as already-validated shortens the path. For dual-use hardware — drones, sensors, effectors — a modeling-and-simulation package (digital twin, synthetic scenarios, hardware-in-the-loop results) lets you demonstrate performance against the mission without waiting for a live range. Reference it in the brief and bring it to pitch day; evaluators reward teams who arrive with evidence instead of promises.

Write for a busy human

Short sentences. Real numbers. One idea per paragraph. Include a one-line company overview and a summary of fundraising to date or top-line revenue — the CSO guidance explicitly asks for it. Cut adjectives, cut backstory, cut anything that does not help an evaluator say “yes, invite them.” The CSO guidebook and DIU's own best-practices page are the ground truth — read them before you write a word.

After you submit

Selection means a pitch to DIU technical staff and the actual military end user, where live demos carry real weight. If you advance, you write the full proposal. So treat the brief as the audition it is: earn the pitch, and let the pitch earn the contract.

Official sources: DIU — Submit a Solution · DIU CSO Guidebook 2026 (PDF) · DIU — Commercial. Figures change; confirm on the official page before relying on them.
