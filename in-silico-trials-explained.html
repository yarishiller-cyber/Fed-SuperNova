In-silico trials explained: virtual patients, real approvals

Updated July 29, 2026 · Free educational guide · verify details at the official sources below

An in-silico trial evaluates a device on a virtual cohort — simulated patients with realistic anatomical and physiological variation — instead of, or alongside, live enrollment. The FDA promotes computational modeling for this purpose, and real programs (HeartFlow's CFD diagnostic, the FDA–Dassault ENRICHMENT study) show virtual patients supplementing or guiding clinical evidence.

The idea

A traditional clinical trial enrolls real patients, which is slow, expensive, and ethically fraught when a device is unproven. An in-silico trial runs the device against a virtual cohort: computational models of patients that reproduce the anatomical and physiological variability of the real target population. You test the device across that spread of virtual anatomies and read out performance and safety before — or alongside — human enrollment.

This is not science fiction

The FDA's device center actively promotes computational modeling for this purpose through its regulatory-science programs. Concrete examples exist: HeartFlow turned patient-specific coronary CFD into an FDA-cleared, reimbursed diagnostic. The FDA's collaboration with Dassault Systèmes on the Living Heart ENRICHMENT study used virtual patients to guide a mitral-clip device evaluation. Regulators are leaning in.

What a virtual patient is made of

Each virtual patient is a physics model — geometry from imaging, tissue material properties, and boundary conditions like blood pressure or breathing. You generate a population by varying those inputs across clinically realistic ranges. The device is then simulated in each: a stent deploying into hundreds of artery shapes, a valve seating against a spread of annulus sizes, an antenna radiating through different body habitus.

Where it helps most

In-silico evidence rarely replaces a trial outright today, but it can shrink one — reducing the number of enrolled patients needed for statistical power, covering rare anatomies that are hard to recruit, and exposing failure modes before they reach a human. That's a direct hit on the two things that kill device startups: time and cash burn.

The honest limitations

In-silico evidence is only as good as the model behind it, and a virtual cohort can't invent biology you didn't put in. If your model doesn't capture a failure mode — an unexpected tissue response, a manufacturing variation — the simulation won't warn you about it. Regulators know this, which is why the credibility bar scales with how much the model drives the decision. Treat in-silico results as a way to sharpen and de-risk a clinical program, not as a magic exemption from it.

What it takes to run one

You need validated models (see FDA V&V40), a defensible way to build the cohort, and real compute — hundreds of runs mean HPC, not a laptop. Qualifying the underlying model through the FDA's MDDT program lets you reuse it across submissions, which is where the economics start to favor teams that invested early.
