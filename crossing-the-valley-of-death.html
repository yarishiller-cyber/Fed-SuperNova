Crossing the valley of death in defense tech

Updated July 29, 2026 · Free educational guide · verify details at the official sources below

In defense tech, the 'valley of death' is the gap between a proven prototype (often SBIR-funded) and sustained procurement in a program of record. Companies die there because there's no money to build at scale. The tools to cross it: APFIT production buys, DoD Office of Strategic Capital loans, DPA Title III/IBAS industrial-base investment, and SSBCI state capital — used together, not alone.

What the valley actually is

The 'valley of death' isn't a metaphor for one bad quarter. It's a structural gap. You win SBIR or a prototype OTA, you build something that works, a warfighter likes it — and then nothing. There's no line of funding to buy it in quantity, and a formal program of record is years away. Your burn keeps climbing while revenue doesn't. That's where good companies with real capabilities quietly fail.

Record private defense-tech co-investment in 2026 has helped, but private money alone rarely bridges a hardware ramp. You need the government tools that were built specifically for this gap.

The four tools that bridge it

APFIT buys production units of your proven tech — non-dilutive procurement, averaging over $30M, that gets your product into the field and keeps you alive. Remember: you can't apply, you get nominated.

The Office of Strategic Capital lends $10M–$150M to build the factory and buy the equipment behind those units — debt, via NOFAs.

DPA Title III and IBAS invest to close documented industrial-base shortfalls — capacity, materials, and single-source risks — often through the Defense Industrial Base Consortium. SSBCI is state-level capital that can be your quiet first check.

Why timing kills more companies than technology

The cruel thing about the valley is that it's rarely about the tech. Your product works — that's why a warfighter wants it. What kills you is the calendar. A program of record can take three to five years to stand up funding lines; your runway is measured in months. APFIT exists to compress that gap, but only if you've lined up a sponsor before your cash runs out. Founders who start the relationship the quarter they need money have already lost. Start it the year before.

There's also a diligence-timeline trap. An OSC loan is real project finance — pre-application consultation, Application Part 1, then months of underwriting. If you wait until you're desperate to apply, the loan closes long after the crisis. The winners run these processes in parallel with delivery, not in sequence after it.

Sequence beats any single program

The teams that cross the valley don't rely on one program. They stack: SBIR to prove it, an internal sponsor to want it, APFIT to buy it, OSC or DPA to build it, SSBCI to fill gaps. And they de-risk the engineering in parallel, because the fastest way to fall back into the valley is a production ramp that misses yield or fails qualification.
