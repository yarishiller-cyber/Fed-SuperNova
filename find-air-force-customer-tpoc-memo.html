Customer

How to find your Air Force customer and the memo that unlocks Phase II

Updated July 29, 2026 · Free educational guide · verify details at the official sources below

AFWERX Open Topic Phase II and Direct-to-Phase-II proposals require a customer memorandum signed by a Department of the Air Force customer, an end-user, and a Technical Point of Contact (TPOC). The TPOC must be DAF civilian or military. You find them by networking at pitch days and conferences, working AFWERX and SpaceWERX matchmaking, and cold-reaching program offices whose mission your technology serves.

The single biggest difference between a startup that stalls at Phase I and one that reaches STRATFI is a real Air Force customer. AFWERX designed the program around it: Open Topic Phase II and Direct-to-Phase-II (D2P2) proposals require a customer memorandum that documents genuine government interest. No memo, no award.

Who you actually need

The memo names three roles. The customer is the Department of the Air Force or Joint organization that wants the capability. The end-user is the operator who would actually use it. The TPOC — Technical Point of Contact — is the government technical sponsor, and this person must be DAF civilian or military. Sometimes one person wears two hats, but you cannot invent them; evaluators verify.

Where to find them

  • Pitch days and conferences. Events like the AFA Warfare Symposium and Space Symposium put program offices and operators in one building. Go with a one-line description of the problem you solve.
  • AFWERX and SpaceWERX matchmaking. The programs actively connect companies to government stakeholders — use those channels rather than guessing.
  • Warm intros from your Phase I. Your Phase I TPOC already knows the mission landscape. Ask who else feels this pain.
  • Targeted cold outreach. Identify the wing, squadron, or program office whose mission your tech serves and reach out with a specific, respectful ask.
If your project needs simulation

The fastest way to earn a busy program officer's signature is to make their decision easy: show them the capability working. A simulation that models your system against their mission scenario turns an abstract pitch into something a TPOC can defend up their chain. Ansys evaluation licensing helps small teams build exactly that artifact.

Completing the memorandum

AFWERX publishes a step-by-step guide to the form — read it before you draft anything. The memo should state, in the customer's own words, the mission gap, why your solution matters, and how they intend to use or transition it. Vague enthusiasm reads as a favor; a specific operational need reads as a market. Give your sponsor a clean draft to react to so you are not asking a busy officer to write from scratch.

Start this earlier than feels comfortable. Government signatures move at government speed, and a customer memo often needs a sponsor to socialize it up their own chain before they will sign. Founders who wait until the proposal window opens routinely miss the deadline not because no one was interested, but because the interested person could not get approval in time. Treat customer discovery as a months-long sales motion that runs in parallel with your technical work, not a form you fill out at the end.

Why this compounds

A signed customer memo is not paperwork — it is the seed of everything downstream. The same relationship that unlocks Phase II is what makes you competitive for STRATFI and TACFI matching funds and, eventually, a sole-source Phase III contract. Invest in the customer first; the funding follows the mission fit.
