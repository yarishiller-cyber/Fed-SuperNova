Who gets funded: how the reviewers actually choose

Updated July 29, 2026 · Free educational guide · verify details at the official sources below

NIH scores applications on significance, innovation, approach, investigators, and environment, with commercialization potential weighing heavily for SBIR. ARPA-H rewards bold, milestone-driven proposals with a credible path to a defined outcome. Across both, the funded device shows a real clinical need, a de-risked technical plan, and a team that can execute — not just a clever idea.

Two cultures, one bar: credible ambition

NIH and ARPA-H feel different in a room, but they filter for the same thing — a bold idea backed by evidence it could actually work. Understanding how each scores you keeps you from writing the wrong proposal for the right agency.

How NIH scores

NIH study sections rate five criteria: significance (does this matter clinically?), innovation (is it genuinely new?), approach (is the plan sound and de-risked?), investigators (can this team do it?), and environment (do you have the resources?). For SBIR, commercialization potential is a heavy additional weight — reviewers want to see a market, a buyer, and a business that survives after the grant. Weak 'Approach' scores sink more device applications than weak ideas do.

How ARPA-H decides

ARPA-H program managers act more like mission-driven investors. They fund aggressive, milestone-gated work toward a specific breakthrough the program was created to achieve. 'Incremental but safe' loses here; 'ambitious but unfounded' also loses. The winner is ambitious and grounded — a hard target with a believable line to it.

If your project needs simulation

The fastest way to lift your 'Approach' score is to show, not assert, that the physics works. An early simulation result — via an Ansys eval — does exactly that.

What separates funded from filed

Three things, over and over. First, a real clinical need with evidence of demand — this is where I-Corps customer discovery pays off. Second, a de-risked technical approach: preliminary data, and increasingly, early simulation results that show the physics is on your side. Third, a team that has clearly done the homework — the right institute, a conversation with the program officer, a coherent regulatory plan.

Common reasons device applications get dinged

Patterns repeat across study sections. The clinical need is asserted but not evidenced — no letters of interest, no market data, no interviews. The aims are really one aim dressed up as three, so a single failure kills the whole project. The commercialization plan is a paragraph of hand-waving about a huge addressable market with no path to a first customer. And the feasibility rests entirely on 'we'll figure it out in Phase I' with no preliminary data. Any one of these can drop you below the funding line even when the idea is good.

The regulatory plan reviewers want to see

You don't need a cleared device to apply, but you do need to show you understand the path. Naming your likely pathway — 510(k), De Novo, or PMA — and acknowledging the predicate or the novel-classification reality signals maturity. It tells the reviewer you won't burn the award and then discover a regulatory wall you never planned for.

The engineering-evidence edge

Most first-time device teams under-invest in showing feasibility. A single credible finite-element or CFD figure in your preliminary data changes how a reviewer reads the whole application: this team can execute. Pair that with a clear credibility plan and you're answering the reviewer's unspoken question — will this actually work — before they ask it.
