Prototype to production: the follow-on OT that changes everything

Updated July 29, 2026 · Free educational guide · verify details at the official sources below

A successfully completed prototype OT can, by statute, convert to a production or follow-on OT without a new competition. That non-competitive path from prototype to scale is the single biggest reason startups pursue OTA — it's a real exit from the pilot-purgatory that traps most defense innovation.

Why this is the whole point

Most defense innovation dies in the 'valley of death' — a successful pilot that never scales because production requires a fresh, brutal re-competition. OTA was built to fix exactly this. Under the statute (10 U.S.C. 4022), a prototype OT that's successfully completed can lead to a follow-on production OT awarded without further competition.

The conditions

Two things generally have to be true: the prototype was competitively awarded in the first place, and it was successfully completed as agreed. Meet those, and the government can transition you straight to production. MTEC, for instance, states follow-on work is expected to be awarded non-competitively to the original awardee based on results and government need.

Set it up from day one

Transition isn't automatic — you engineer for it. In your original white paper and proposal, name the program that would field this and describe the production path. Deliver your prototype milestones cleanly. Keep the program office and consortium sponsor close during execution. The teams that transition are the ones who treated the prototype as step one, not the finish line.

If your project needs simulation

Production reliability is a simulation problem — fatigue, tolerances, thermal margin at rate. An Ansys eval through a partner helps you carry feasibility all the way to manufacturability.

What changes at production scale

The engineering questions shift. A prototype asks 'does it work?'; production asks 'does it work every time, at rate, for years?' That means reliability, manufacturability, tolerance stack-ups and sustainment — and it's why simulation stays valuable long after the demo. Curious how teams got here?
