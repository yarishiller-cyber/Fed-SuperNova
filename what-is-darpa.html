What is DARPA, and how does it fund deep-tech startups?

Updated July 29, 2026 · Free educational guide · verify details at the official sources below

DARPA is the U.S. Department of Defense's advanced-research agency, with a budget of roughly $4B a year spread across six technical offices. It funds high-risk, high-payoff research mostly through non-dilutive contracts, other transactions, and cooperative agreements — it takes no equity. Startups, universities, and primes all compete on the same footing.

The agency that funds the impossible

DARPA — the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency — exists to make strategic bets on technologies that look impossible today and reshape national security tomorrow. It has done this for decades, seeding early work behind the internet, stealth, GPS, autonomy, and mRNA medicine. For a founder, the important point is simpler: DARPA pays for hard R&D, and it does not take your equity.

Six offices, one mission

DARPA organizes its work into six technical offices, each owning a slice of the hard-problem space: the Defense Sciences Office (foundational science and materials), the microsystems office (microelectronics and photonics — recently reorganized), the Strategic Technology Office (sensors, EW, autonomy), the Tactical Technology Office (platforms, space, weapons), the Biological Technologies Office, and the information/computing office. A separate Commercial Strategy Office runs commercialization programs like EEI. Knowing which office owns your problem tells you which program managers to talk to.

How the money actually moves

DARPA's ~$4B annual budget flows out through Broad Agency Announcements, office-wide BAAs, fast seedling-style Disruption Opportunities, SBIR/STTR set-asides, and other-transaction agreements. Almost all of it is non-dilutive. A full program award can run $5M–$50M to a performer team; a seedling can be a few hundred thousand dollars to prove one idea is feasible.

Who can win

This is the part founders underestimate: DARPA is genuinely open. A two-person stealth team, a university lab, and a defense prime can all respond to the same BAA. What DARPA cares about is whether you can hit the technical metric a program manager has defined — not how big your company is. Small businesses also get dedicated set-asides through SBIR and STTR.

The catch: you must convince a skeptic

Every DARPA program has a program manager whose job is to be excited but skeptical. They have seen a hundred confident pitches for things that violate physics. Winning means showing your idea is aggressive and credible — which is why the strongest proposals carry real engineering evidence, not just ambition.
