Launch access

The CubeSat Launch Initiative: NASA's (nearly) free ride to orbit

Updated July 29, 2026 · Free educational guide · verify details at the official sources below

The CubeSat Launch Initiative (CSLI) selects small satellites from U.S. educational institutions, non-profits, and NASA centers and manifests them onto launches — bundled into ELaNa missions — at no launch cost to the developer. You apply through an Announcement of Partnership Opportunity, historically released around August with proposals due in November. You still fund and build the satellite; NASA covers the ride.

The cheapest way to get a small satellite to orbit is to not pay for the launch — and for U.S. educational institutions, non-profits, and NASA centers, that is exactly what the CubeSat Launch Initiative offers. CSLI has manifested well over 150 CubeSats since it began, giving teams a low-cost pathway to real spaceflight for science and technology demonstrations.

What CSLI actually gives you. NASA's Launch Services Program collects selected CubeSats and manifests them onto upcoming launches — as primary rideshare, secondary payloads, or International Space Station deployments. Each launch carrying these payloads is bundled into an ELaNa mission (Educational Launch of Nanosatellites) with its own number. NASA covers the integration and launch; you do not pay for the ride. That single fact removes the largest cost barrier standing between a university lab or a scrappy non-profit and actual orbit.

Who qualifies. Eligibility historically centers on U.S. educational institutions, non-profit organizations, and NASA centers. Commercial startups are not the core audience — but founders routinely reach orbit through CSLI by partnering with a university lab, which is a legitimate and common path to flight heritage before a commercial mission.

If your project needs simulation

CSLI is not a grant. It buys you the single most expensive line item — the launch — but not the spacecraft. You still need money to design, build, license (FCC/NOAA), and operate the CubeSat. Pair CSLI with a build-funding source and you have a full path to orbit; treat it as a grant and you'll get selected with nothing to fly.

The application cycle. CSLI opportunities come through an Announcement of Partnership Opportunity, historically released around August with proposals due in November. NASA has signalled updates for future CSLI cycles, so confirm the current window on the official page rather than assuming last year's dates hold. Selection does not guarantee an immediate launch — you enter a queue and get manifested when a suitable ride appears, which can be a wait of a year or more.

What a strong proposal looks like. NASA is buying educational and scientific value, so lead with the investigation: what does this CubeSat measure or demonstrate, who learns from it, and why does it need to be in space rather than on a bench. Show that the team can realistically build and operate the spacecraft, and that you have — or have a plan for — the FCC or NOAA licensing that a real mission requires.

Where it fits in your funding ladder. Treat CSLI as the launch line of your budget, not the whole budget. Combine it with a build-funding source and campus resources and you have a complete route to orbit for a fraction of the commercial price. Start at the official CSLI page and read how ELaNa missions are assembled so your proposal speaks the program's language.

Official sources: NASA CubeSat Launch Initiative · NASA ELaNa — Educational Launch of Nanosatellites. Figures change; confirm on the official page before relying on them.
